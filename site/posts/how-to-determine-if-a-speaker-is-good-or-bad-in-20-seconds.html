How to Tell if a Speaker Is Strong or Weak — in Just 20 Seconds

By Wade Paterson

First impressions are notoriously difficult to change, and public speaking is no exception. An audience will judge whether a speaker is strong or weak within the first few seconds of watching them on stage. The good news? The signals that separate polished professionals from nervous amateurs are well-known — and entirely within your control. Here are five telling indicators that reveal a speaker's skill level almost immediately, along with practical advice for strengthening each one.

1. What They Do with Their Eyes

Eye contact is one of the fastest ways to distinguish a confident speaker from an uncertain one. A weak speaker tends to rely on notes, staring down at a lectern and rarely engaging the audience visually. A slightly stronger speaker may step away from the lectern but will still glance up and down frequently — looking down out of a lack of confidence, or looking upward while visibly trying to recall their next point.

Strong speakers, by contrast, are intentional with their eye contact. They scan the room and make a point of connecting with individuals throughout the audience. Eye contact creates accountability — when an audience member sees you looking directly at them, they're far more likely to stay engaged.

The most skilled speakers take this a step further: they finish a thought while maintaining eye contact with a single person. Rather than constantly sweeping the room, they lock in with one individual, deliver a complete point, and then shift their gaze to someone new as they move on to the next idea. It's a subtle technique with a powerful effect.

2. What They Do with Their Feet

Nervous energy has a way of traveling straight to a speaker's lower body. Amateur speakers often sway their weight back and forth, lean to one side, tap a foot, or pace without purpose. Sometimes they don't even realize they're doing it — but the audience notices, and it signals discomfort.

Strong speakers stay planted. They're calm and grounded. That doesn't mean they stand perfectly still for the entire presentation — they absolutely move — but their movement is deliberate. They'll hold their position while completing a thought, then physically transition to a new spot on the stage as they shift to a new idea. Their movement reinforces their message rather than distracting from it.

If you're a newer speaker, try recording yourself during a presentation. Watch for unconscious habits like swaying, leaning, or crossing your legs. Once you're aware of them, you can work on keeping your feet steady and moving only with purpose.

3. What They Do with Their Hands

Hands are another immediate tell. Weaker speakers gravitate toward a lectern and grip it tightly, using it as a security blanket. Others bury their hands in their pockets or cross their arms — anything to avoid the vulnerability of having their hands visible and unoccupied.

Strong speakers use their hands to engage the audience. More importantly, they're comfortable letting their hands rest naturally at their sides when they're not gesturing, then bringing them up purposefully to emphasize a point before letting them fall again.

One common habit to watch for is what's known as the invisible table — keeping the arms locked at a 90-degree angle and gesturing only above waist level, never letting the hands drop. While it's better than gripping a lectern, it still looks rigid and unnatural. If you recognize this habit in yourself, consciously break through that invisible barrier. Let your arms relax at your sides between gestures. It's a small change that instantly makes you appear more polished and at ease.

4. How They Open Their Speech

Content matters from the very first sentence. Consider this common opening:

"Hey everyone, my name is Wade. Today I'm going to be talking to you about social media. We're going to go over a few different tips and tricks that I found are beneficial."

It's not terrible, but it's generic — and it signals that the speaker hasn't put much thought into their opening. Elite speakers skip the formalities and jump straight into something compelling. They have three go-to strategies:

  • A thought-provoking question: "If you could only be on one social media network, which one would you choose?"
  • A powerful statistic: "Did you know 80% of videos on social media are viewed without sound? If you're not including subtitles, only 20% of your audience is getting the full message."
  • A personal story: "When I was in 11th grade, I had my first experience with social media — and it became one of my most embarrassing moments."

Each of these approaches delivers value immediately, disrupts the audience's expectations, and creates curiosity. This is what separates amateurs from professionals: the ability to grab attention in the first few seconds rather than wasting them on introductions the audience doesn't need.

5. How They Handle Silence (and Filler Words)

Few things expose an inexperienced speaker faster than a flood of filler words. Um, uh, so, basically, like — these sounds and words get wedged between sentences without adding any value. Remove them and the meaning of your speech wouldn't change at all. We use them because standing in front of a room full of people makes silence feel unbearable, so we instinctively fill every gap with noise.

Polished speakers understand that silence is not the enemy. In fact, a well-placed pause is more enjoyable to listen to than a string of filler words. Pauses give the audience time to absorb what you've said and create a sense of confidence and control.

Eliminating filler words is a two-step process:

  • Step one: Identify your fillers. Record yourself speaking or attend a Toastmasters meeting, where a designated "ah counter" will track and report your filler words. You can't fix what you don't notice.
  • Step two: Replace fillers with silence. Once you start catching yourself mid-filler, don't panic. Simply note it and begin substituting a brief pause instead. You're replacing an old habit with a new one — and the new one will make you sound dramatically more polished and professional.

The Takeaway

An audience forms its opinion of a speaker remarkably quickly — often within the first 20 seconds. The signals they read are not about talent or charisma; they're about intentionality. Purposeful eye contact, grounded feet, natural hand gestures, a compelling opening, and the confident use of silence all communicate the same message: this person knows what they're doing. The best part is that every one of these skills is learnable. Record yourself, identify the habits that undermine your presence, and start replacing them with deliberate, polished alternatives. Small adjustments in these five areas can transform the way an audience perceives you — before you've even made your first major point.
