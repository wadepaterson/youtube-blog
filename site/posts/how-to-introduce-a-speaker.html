How to Introduce a Speaker: Tips for a Polished, Energising Handoff

By Wade Paterson

We've all seen it happen. Someone walks on stage, clutches a piece of paper, and proceeds to read a speaker's bio word for word in a flat, monotone voice. No eye contact. No energy. No connection with the audience. It's an underwhelming start that does a disservice to the speaker who's about to take the stage — and it's far more common than it should be. Whether you're an event sponsor, a company executive, or a fellow presenter, introducing a speaker is a genuine opportunity to set the tone for what's ahead. Here's how to do it well.

Stop Reading the Bio Verbatim

When you're tasked with introducing a speaker, it's tempting to read their bio exactly as it was sent to the event organiser. Every degree, every accomplishment, every line — delivered word for word from a sheet of paper. Resist that temptation.

Most speakers would far rather have an energetic, warm introduction that misses one or two credentials than a robotic recitation that hits every bullet point but lands with all the excitement of a tax return. Your goal isn't to be a human teleprompter. It's to make the audience excited about what's coming next.

This means you need to internalise the key information and deliver it conversationally — as if you were telling a group of friends about an incredible speaker you once saw. You can still keep a few bullet points nearby for reference, but your default should be speaking to the audience, not reading at them. Use all the fundamentals of good public speaking: smile, make eye contact, vary your tone, and use open body language.

If You Must Use Notes, Use Them Wisely

Ideally, you'll introduce the speaker without holding any notes at all. But if you absolutely need a safety net, avoid bringing a single loose sheet of paper on stage. Here's why: nerves often cause shaky hands, and a fluttering piece of paper is very noticeable to an audience. It broadcasts your anxiety and can be embarrassing.

A simple workaround is to place your notes on something heavier — a clipboard or a sturdy notepad. This serves two purposes:

  • It looks more professional. Walking on stage with a clipboard feels intentional. A crumpled sheet of paper does not.
  • It minimises visible shaking. The added weight makes any trembling in your hands far less noticeable.

It's a small adjustment that can make a big difference in how composed you appear.

Two Things to Avoid at All Costs

There are two common mistakes that can undermine even a well-prepared speaker introduction. Be mindful of both.

1. Don't Make It About Yourself

You might not plan to do this, but it happens more often than you'd think. The introducer takes the stage and begins reminiscing about their own career, their own speaking journey, and how they've known the speaker for years. Suddenly, what should be a sixty-second setup turns into a self-congratulatory monologue.

Remember: this moment isn't about you. Your job is to honour the speaker, shine the spotlight on them, and give the audience the context they need to appreciate what's coming. Keep yourself out of the narrative as much as possible.

2. Don't Overhype the Speaker

It might seem generous to tell the audience they're about to witness "the best speech of the entire conference, without question!" but this is a trap. When you set expectations impossibly high, you make it nearly impossible for the speaker to deliver on the promise you've made on their behalf.

Think of it like watching a film. If someone tells you a movie is going to be the greatest thing you've ever seen, you're almost guaranteed to be disappointed. But if you go in with reasonable expectations and are pleasantly surprised, the experience feels remarkable.

You don't need to undersell the speaker — just present their credentials, explain what they'll be talking about, and offer a warm welcome. Something like, "Please help me give a warm welcome to…" is far more effective than breathless superlatives.

Practice Like It's a Real Speech

It's easy to dismiss a one-minute introduction as something that doesn't require preparation. After all, you're only on stage briefly before handing things over. But that attitude almost always leads to regret.

Even a short introduction requires rehearsal. You need to practise delivering the speaker's key information naturally, without leaning on your notes. The goal is to communicate their background and credentials as though you're sharing information you already know — not discovering it for the first time alongside the audience.

Practice is one of the most important elements of public speaking, period. That principle doesn't disappear just because your segment is sixty seconds long.

Give the Speaker a Warm Handoff

A great introduction doesn't end when you say the speaker's name. It ends when the transition is seamless and the stage feels alive. Here's how to make that happen:

  • Talk to the speaker beforehand. Introduce yourself, let them know you'll be bringing them on, and walk them through your plan. Confirm that the information you have is current — bios can become outdated quickly.
  • Agree on the logistics. Let them know you'll say their name, wait on stage, shake their hand as they arrive, and then exit. This eliminates any awkward gaps.
  • Keep the stage warm. Don't rush off the moment you finish speaking. Stay in place until the speaker reaches you. If there's music playing, let it build while you wait. A handshake or a brief, confident gesture of welcome goes a long way.
  • Don't forget the clicker. If the event uses presentation slides and you've been holding the clicker, make sure you hand it off smoothly. It's a small detail, but fumbling over AV equipment in front of an audience can break the momentum you've just built.

This entire exchange may take only a few seconds, but it signals professionalism and respect — both for the speaker and the audience.

Bringing It All Together

Introducing a speaker is a small but meaningful responsibility. Done well, it energises the room, honours the speaker, and sets the stage for a memorable presentation. Done poorly, it creates an awkward, flat start that the speaker has to work twice as hard to overcome. The formula is straightforward: internalise the key information, deliver it with warmth and energy, avoid making it about yourself or overhyping the moment, practise your delivery, and execute a smooth, professional handoff. Treat those one or two minutes on stage with the same care you'd give any speech — because to the audience and the speaker alike, those moments matter more than you might think.
