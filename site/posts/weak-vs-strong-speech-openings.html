How to Open a Speech Like a Pro: Replace Weak Openings with Powerful Ones

By Wade Paterson

Every speech begins with a first impression — and most speakers waste it. Whether it's a wedding toast, a corporate presentation, or a conference keynote, the opening seconds of your speech are prime real estate for capturing attention. Yet time and again, speakers squander those moments on apologies, microphone tests, and unnecessary introductions. The good news? With a few simple shifts, you can transform a forgettable opening into one that hooks your audience from the very first sentence.

Stop Introducing Yourself When You've Already Been Introduced

Picture a wedding reception. The MC has just introduced the best man by name. Programs on every table list the wedding party. And yet, the best man steps up to the microphone and says: "For those who don't know me, my name is Wade, and I'm the best man."

It's one of the most common speech openings — and one of the weakest. The audience already knows who you are and why you're speaking. Restating that information burns valuable seconds and does nothing to draw people in.

Instead, try something like this: "Today, I am Jeremy's best man. But in fifth grade, we were arch enemies."

Suddenly, every guest in the room is leaning forward. There's a story here. They thought these two were always close friends — what happened in fifth grade? You've created intrigue, and the audience is hooked. Disrupt the expected pattern. Share a story, offer a surprising fact, or ask a thought-provoking question. These are the tools that capture attention far more effectively than a redundant introduction.

Never Open with an Apology

Few things undermine a speaker's credibility faster than beginning with: "I have to apologize — I'm not really a public speaker. I'm so nervous right now, so bear with me."

It's understandable. Public speaking — formally known as glossophobia — is consistently ranked as one of the greatest personal fears in the world. But if you've been asked to deliver a presentation, someone believes in your expertise. You were chosen for a reason. Starting with an apology signals to the audience that they should lower their expectations, and they may take less value from everything you say afterward.

The solution isn't to pretend your nerves don't exist — it's to prepare thoroughly enough that confidence replaces anxiety. Practice your speech multiple times so that by the day of delivery, you can open with strength rather than self-deprecation.

Consider the difference. Instead of apologising, try opening with a powerful fact: "Did you know that 80% of videos on social media are viewed without the sound on? If you're creating video content without subtitles or captions, 80% of your audience may not be getting your message."

An opening like this delivers immediate value. It positions you as a knowledgeable expert. And even if butterflies are raging inside, your audience will never know.

Run Better Meetings with Intentional Openings

Weak openings aren't limited to formal speeches — they plague meetings too. How many times have you sat through a meeting that begins with something like: "Okay, we have the annual fundraiser coming up. Does anyone have any ideas for what we can do this year?"

These vague, open-ended questions lobbed into the group without structure are a recipe for awkward silence — or for the same one or two people dominating the conversation while everyone else disengages. This dynamic is especially pronounced in virtual meetings, where the barrier to speaking up feels even higher.

A better approach? Open with a bold, provocative statement that sets the tone: "This year, we're going to double the amount of funds raised from last year."

That kind of ambitious declaration immediately shifts the energy in the room. People start thinking about how rather than whether. Then, follow up with targeted, specific questions directed at individual team members:

  • "Brett, you led the organizing committee last year. Share with the group what went well and where we could improve."
  • "Angela, you're new to the team — based on what Brett just said, what fresh ideas do you have for doubling our results?"

By directing questions to specific people and tailoring them to each person's experience or perspective, you create a more dynamic, inclusive, and productive meeting. You eliminate the long pauses, encourage maximum participation, and set a tone of purposeful energy from the very start.

Skip the Microphone Test — Do It Before You Speak

There are two microphone-related openings that kill momentum every time:

  • "Hello? Whoa — this thing is LOUD!"
  • "Hello? Can everyone hear me? Can you hear me in the back?"

In the first case, you shouldn't be surprised that a microphone amplifies your voice — that is its entire purpose. In the second, while ensuring audibility is considerate, it makes you come across as unprepared and wastes those critical first seconds on generic housekeeping.

The solution is simple: do a mic check before the event begins. If one is offered, take full advantage of it. If it's not offered, ask the event organizer: "Is there a chance I can get on stage early to familiarise myself with the microphone?" Find out whether you'll be using a handheld or a lapel mic. Hear your voice projected into the room. Confirm with someone at the back that the volume is right.

Once you've completed this preparation, you no longer need to test the mic in front of your audience. Instead, you can step up and jump straight into a compelling story, a thought-provoking question, or a powerful statistic — immediately commanding the room.

Detach from the Lectern

We've all seen it: a speaker steps up, places their notes on the lectern, grips the sides, and proceeds to read their speech to the audience with minimal eye contact and a monotone cadence. Even the occasional glance upward tends to land on the same spot in the room. The result? The audience mentally checks out.

Relying heavily on a lectern and reading from notes signals to your audience that your public speaking abilities are limited. It creates a physical and psychological barrier between you and the people you're trying to reach.

Here's what to do instead:

  • Turn the lectern diagonally. If you need notes, position them at an angle so you can step away and return to them naturally rather than hiding behind the podium.
  • Use bullet points, not full scripts. The best speakers don't use notes at all, but if you're not quite there yet, brief bullet points allow you to stay on track without reading verbatim.
  • Free your body. When you detach from the lectern, you unlock the ability to use vocal variety, purposeful hand gestures, and movement — all of which dramatically increase audience engagement.
  • Make eye contact. Lock eyes with individuals throughout the room. This creates connection and makes your message feel personal and direct.

The ultimate goal is to deliver your speech without relying on any notes at all. When you can do that — when you're free to use your voice, your body language, and your eye contact fully — your speech becomes infinitely more engaging and memorable. And a memorable speech means your message actually lands. The thing you were trying to teach, persuade, or inspire stays with your audience long after you've left the stage.

The Openings That Work

Across all of these scenarios — weddings, presentations, meetings, and conference talks — the strongest openings share a few common traits. They skip the formalities and get straight to the substance. They surprise the audience with something unexpected. And they create curiosity that compels people to keep listening. Here's a quick summary of techniques that consistently work:

  • Tell a story. Nothing hooks an audience like a narrative with tension or surprise.
  • Share a surprising fact or statistic. Immediate value positions you as an expert and makes people pay attention.
  • Ask a thought-provoking question. Engage your audience's minds from the very first moment.
  • Make a bold statement. Set an ambitious tone that energises the room and frames everything that follows.

The opening of your speech sets the trajectory for everything that follows. A weak start forces you to spend the rest of your time trying to win back attention you never captured in the first place. A strong start, on the other hand, gives you momentum, credibility, and an audience that's genuinely engaged. So the next time you step up to speak — whether it's in a boardroom, at a wedding, or on a stage — resist the urge to apologise, test the mic, or restate your name. Instead, open with something your audience won't forget. That's where great speeches begin.
