How to Give a Toastmasters Speech Evaluation: A Complete Guide with Real-World Examples

By Wade Paterson

Giving a speech evaluation in Toastmasters can feel almost as nerve-wracking as delivering a speech itself. You have just two to three minutes to offer meaningful feedback that celebrates what a speaker did well while gently guiding them toward improvement. How do you structure your thoughts on the fly? What should you write down? And how do you deliver your evaluation with confidence? In this guide, I'll walk you through the entire process — from watching a live speech to delivering the evaluation to breaking down the exact note-taking method that makes it all possible.

The Speech: "Speaking Anyway" by Samantha Hildebrand

To illustrate the evaluation process, let's start with the speech itself. Samantha Hildebrand, an award-winning Toastmaster from the Kelowna Toastmasters Club in British Columbia, delivered a speech titled Speaking Anyway — a talk about overcoming the nerves and anxiety that so many of us experience before public speaking.

Samantha opened by asking the audience to think of a moment where they felt anxious — a job interview, an uncomfortable conversation, or being called to the front of a room to give a presentation. She then shared her own journey, recalling how her knees would buckle, her hands would shake, and her words would race when she first started at Toastmasters. Evaluators would tell her to slow down and challenge herself just to hit the one-minute green light.

From there, Samantha walked the audience through four practical tools for managing speaking anxiety:

  • Practice: She shared a vivid story from a Tall Tales contest semifinal where she couldn't remember the second line of her speech while waiting backstage — but when her name was called, the words flowed because she had rehearsed her speech over a hundred times in front of family, friends, and fellow Toastmasters.
  • Visualize: She guided the audience through a mental rehearsal — walking confidently to the front of the room, delivering the speech clearly, using open body language, and receiving applause at the end. "When you take a moment to picture what it is that you want," she said, "that becomes your reality."
  • Breathe: She led the room through a 4-second inhale and 4-second exhale exercise, demonstrating how even a single round of intentional breathing can provide relief from nerves.
  • Move: She got the entire audience on their feet, shaking out their arms, rolling their wrists, tilting their heads, and shaking their legs — releasing the tension the body holds before a speech.

She closed by reminding everyone that nerves before speaking are completely normal and that a little adrenaline actually helps make your delivery more energetic. The key is to practice, visualize, breathe, move — and get up there and speak anyway.

The Evaluation: How I Assessed Samantha's Speech

Now, here's the evaluation I delivered at that same club meeting, clocking in at two minutes and fifty-eight seconds — just under the three-minute maximum. The structure follows a pattern that every evaluator can learn from.

I opened with a summary of the speech's purpose: Samantha taught us how to be tenacious in overcoming speaking anxiety, and who better to learn from than an award-winning Toastmaster from our own club?

Then I highlighted three things she did well:

  • A strong introduction: There are several ways to capture an audience's attention — a thought-provoking question, a powerful statistic, a story, or audience participation. Samantha chose to ask the audience to think of a time they felt anxious, immersing everyone in the topic right away.
  • Excellent pacing and pausing: A specific example of this was when she asked, "How do you relax? You breathe." Then she walked us through the breathing exercise, giving the audience a practical tool they could use in the future.
  • Outstanding body language and audience engagement: Samantha is always good at using the full stage, but in this speech she went further — getting the entire audience out of their seats to participate in the shaking exercise. It demonstrated her expertise in the subject and made her message more credible.

Then I offered two areas for improvement:

  • Simplify the visual aids: The room's screen was small, so I challenged Samantha to use fewer words on her slides — perhaps just a single keyword in 100-point text to cue the audience rather than full sentences to read.
  • Start with stronger vocal variety: While Samantha's vocal energy picked up later in the speech — especially when she recreated the moment her name was called at the contest — the opening was a bit quiet and harder to hear. Starting with more vocal power would match the strength of her content from the very first word.

Finally, I closed with a summary: If Samantha continues to leverage her powerful introductions, body language, and pacing while refining her slides and bringing vocal variety from the start, she will keep taking her speeches to the next level.

The Note-Taking Method: Setting Up Your Page Before the Speaker Begins

The secret to a great evaluation isn't having a brilliant memory — it's having a simple, effective system for capturing your thoughts in real time. Here's the exact method I use.

Before the speaker starts, I set up my page with three elements:

  • The Word of the Day: I write it at the top of the page and draw a box around it. For this meeting, the word was tenacity. Having it visible at the top reminds me to weave it into my evaluation naturally, ideally early on.
  • A "Plus" column: This is where I jot down things the speaker does well.
  • A "Minus" column: This is where I note areas for improvement.

That's it. The page is ready before the speaker even opens their mouth. As the speech unfolds, I simply drop observations into the appropriate column.

What to Listen For: The Universal Building Blocks of a Speech

Every speech, no matter who's delivering it, contains common elements that will fall into one column or the other. Think of them as puzzle pieces you're already familiar with:

  • Eye contact
  • Body language
  • Vocal variety
  • Speech structure
  • Use of humor
  • Pacing and pausing
  • Introduction and conclusion

You already know what these puzzle pieces are. The evaluation is simply a matter of connecting the dots: Did the speaker use these elements effectively, or is there room for improvement?

The Power of Specific Examples

Here's the real key to elevating your evaluations from generic to genuinely helpful: find specific examples you can reference.

Instead of saying, "Your pacing was good," I said, "When you asked, 'How do you relax? You breathe,' and then walked us through the exercise — that was excellent pacing and pausing." That specificity tells the speaker exactly what they did right so they can replicate it in the future.

One critical tip: pay close attention to the speaker's very first words. The introduction is one of the most important parts of any speech, and it's also the part your brain is most likely to forget as the speech continues. Have your pen ready to jot down that opening line immediately. This way, you'll have a concrete example to reference when you evaluate their intro — whether it was a strength or an area for growth.

When you have specific examples written down, you can deliver your evaluation with confidence. A quick glance at your notes is all you need. You're not reading off a piece of paper — you're making eye contact with the audience and using your notes as guideposts.

Don't Forget the Summary

This is especially important if you ever find yourself in a speech evaluation contest, but it's a good habit for every evaluation you give. Always end with a summary that ties together the key strengths and the areas for improvement.

In my evaluation of Samantha, I closed by saying: "If Samantha continues to use great body language, powerful introductions, and controlled pacing and pausing — while also simplifying her slides and incorporating vocal variety from the very start — she's going to continue to elevate her speeches."

In a contest setting, the summary is a dedicated section on the judges' scoring sheets. If you skip it, judges are forced to give you zero points in that category — potentially costing you five to ten points automatically. Even outside of a contest, a strong summary leaves the speaker with a clear, memorable takeaway.

Putting It All Together

The ability to listen carefully, identify what works, spot opportunities for growth, and deliver that feedback with warmth and specificity is a skill that extends far beyond the Toastmasters lectern. Whether you're giving feedback to a colleague on a presentation, coaching a team member, or simply becoming a more attentive listener in everyday life, these evaluation skills are remarkably transferable.

To recap the process: set up your page with the Word of the Day and your plus and minus columns before the speech begins. Listen for the universal elements — eye contact, body language, vocal variety, structure, humor, pacing. Capture specific examples, especially the speaker's opening line. Deliver your evaluation with genuine praise for what worked, constructive suggestions for what could be improved, and a concise summary that brings it all together. Do this consistently, and you won't just become a better evaluator — you'll become a better speaker, a better listener, and a more thoughtful communicator in every area of your life.
