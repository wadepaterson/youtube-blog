How to Find Your Best Stories: Four Exercises Every Speaker Needs

By Wade Paterson

The best speeches aren't built on facts alone — they're built on stories, especially personal ones. Yet when it comes time to identify those stories, most people draw a blank. They sit down, stare at the page, and convince themselves they simply don't have anything interesting to share. If that sounds familiar, you're not alone — and you're wrong. You do have powerful stories inside you. You just need the right tools to uncover them. Here are four thought exercises that will help you build a personal story library you can draw from for any speech, presentation, or conversation.

Exercise 1: Mine Your Emotions

The fastest route to your best stories is through your strongest emotions. Think back through your life and focus on six key feelings:

  • Embarrassment — These moments often become your funniest stories, the ones that make audiences laugh and feel connected to you.
  • Pride — Moments of accomplishment carry lessons you can teach others.
  • Fear — Times when you were truly afraid are opportunities to take your audience on a journey through tension and resolution.
  • Surprise — Events that defied all odds or had no business happening make for inherently compelling content.
  • Disappointment — Sharing your low points draws audiences closer and helps them empathise with your experience.
  • Gratefulness — Stories rooted in gratitude leave a positive, lasting impact on your listeners.

For each emotion, sit with it for a moment. Let the feeling guide you backward through your memory. The stories that surface are almost always among your strongest material.

Exercise 2: Identify Firsts, Lasts, and Turning Points

Another powerful way to unlock your personal stories is to think about the chapters of your life — specifically the beginnings, endings, and pivotal moments.

Ask yourself: What was the first time you failed at something? What about your first car, first job, or first date? Now consider the lasts: What was the last time you did something you loved that you haven't done since? And then explore your turning points: What moment changed your outlook on life, or caused you to break a long-held habit?

These milestones are the natural chapter breaks of your life. They carry inherent narrative weight because they mark change — and change is the heartbeat of every great story. When you revisit these moments, stories will emerge naturally.

Exercise 3: Answer Five Revealing Questions

If you're still struggling to surface stories, try answering these five targeted questions:

  • What's a mistake you can laugh about now, but definitely weren't laughing about at the time?
  • What's the best — and worst — advice you've ever received?
  • What has been the luckiest moment of your life?
  • What is one conversation you have never forgotten? Who was it with, and what was it about?
  • When you're at a gathering of family or friends, what story about you always comes up?

Each of these questions is designed to bypass the mental block of "I don't have any good stories" and point you directly toward moments that already resonate — moments other people may already love hearing about.

Exercise 4: Visualise Your Way Back

This final exercise is the most immersive. Close your eyes and picture your childhood home. Start in your bedroom. What posters are on the walls? What does the room look like? Now walk into the hallway. Move to the kitchen. Who's there? What's being cooked? Step outside and stand on the front lawn, staring back at the house. Walk around to the backyard.

Visualisation works because it places you physically — in your mind — back in the spaces where so many of your memories were formed. The moment you see yourself standing in that kitchen or that backyard, memories begin to surface. And where memories surface, stories follow. This exercise is remarkably effective at unlocking moments you haven't thought about in years.

Think of Your Speech as a Puzzle, Not a Blank Page

Speaker Shawn Kanungo once shared a perspective that fundamentally changes how you approach speechwriting. Instead of starting from a blank document and building from scratch, think of your speech as a puzzle. You have ideas, opinions, and insights — those are some of your puzzle pieces. Your personal stories are the others. When you interlock them, the speech assembles itself.

This reframe matters because stories are remembered far more often by audiences than facts alone. A well-placed personal story doesn't just illustrate your point — it makes your point stick.

Build Your Story List Today

Here's your challenge: open the notes app on your phone right now and list at least five of your best personal stories. Use the four exercises above to generate them. But don't stop at five. Make it a living list. Every time something funny, surprising, difficult, or deeply emotional happens in your life, add it. Over time, you might have dozens — even hundreds — of stories at your fingertips.

The next time a speaking opportunity arises — whether it's a keynote, a team meeting, or even a one-on-one conversation — you won't be scrambling for material. You'll simply reach into your list and pull out the perfect story. And that story will help you make a far bigger impact than any statistic or slide ever could.

Your stories are already inside you. You just need to go looking for them.
